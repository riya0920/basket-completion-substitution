WHY THIS TABLE IS SEGMENTED AND THE AGGREGATE IS NOT REPORTED ALONE:
predicting a REORDER is close to free -- the user buys milk every week,
and a model that just replays their history scores well. The NEW-TO-USER
column is the only place discovery happens, and it is where every method
here is dramatically weaker. An aggregate hit-rate is a weighted average
of an easy problem and a hard one, dominated by whichever is more
frequent, and on grocery data that is always the easy one.

Reorder personalisation is the single largest lift on the reorder segment
and buys little or nothing on new-to-user, which is exactly what it
should do -- and is the reason to look at the columns separately rather
than celebrate the aggregate it inflates.

2. THE CONFLATION -- WHAT CO-OCCURRENCE DOES TO SUBSTITUTES
==============================================================================
                    n  mean_cooccurrence    mean_lift  mean_i2v_complement  mean_i2v_similarity
relation                                                                                       
TRUE complements  180          1432.9056       1.6819               0.7191              -0.1586
TRUE substitutes   26             0.0000       0.0000              -2.3138               0.9099
unrelated         300           681.7267       0.8775               0.0654               0.0502

READ THE FIRST TWO COLUMNS AGAINST THE LAST ONE.

Co-occurrence and lift are HIGH for complements and LOW for substitutes --
lower, in fact, than for unrelated pairs. That is not a failure of the
metric, it is the definition: you buy hot dogs AND buns, you buy cola A
OR cola B. Substitutes are ANTI-correlated within a basket.

So any method built on same-basket co-occurrence -- lift, association
rules, a naive item2vec that keeps only one embedding matrix -- ranks
substitutes as the LEAST related items in the catalogue. A grocery
substitution engine built that way would offer, for an out-of-stock cola,
the products that are least like a cola.

The last column is the fix. cos(W_a, W_b) is SECOND-ORDER: it is high when
two items appear in the same KIND of basket, whether or not they ever
appear together. Both colas show up with chips and salsa, so their input
vectors converge even though they never share a basket.

==============================================================================
Labelled set: 166 pairs (26 substitute / 70 complement / 70 neither).

                                  precision_at_n        auc
scorer

ANNOTATION GUIDELINE -- the rules the label set was built from, stated so
a second annotator could reproduce it:
  SUBSTITUTE  if a shopper who wanted A and could not have it would
              accept B as serving the same purpose in the same meal.
              Test: could B occupy A's slot on the shopping list?
  COMPLEMENT  if A and B are bought to be used TOGETHER. Test: does
              having A make you more likely to want B?
  NEITHER     everything else, including same-aisle pairs that are not
              interchangeable (ketchup and mustard are complements of
              hot dogs and of each other, not substitutes for each other).

WHY THE AUC IS 1.0000, AND WHY YOU SHOULD DISCOUNT IT. The generator
enforces ONE PRODUCT PER FAMILY PER BASKET as a hard constraint, so
substitutes co-occur exactly zero times and the separation is perfect by
construction. Real grocery data is much softer: households stock up on
two brands, buy different sizes of the same thing, or contain people with
different preferences, so genuine substitutes DO share baskets sometimes.
The right way to read this section is that the SIGNAL is the correct one
and the effect size is an artifact of the lab. On real data I would
expect the ranking of scorers to survive and the AUC to be well below 1.

HONESTY ABOUT THIS LABEL SET: in this project the labels come from the
generator's planted structure rather than from human annotators, so they
are ground truth for the SIMULATION and not evidence about real shoppers.
On real Instacart data this set would have to be hand-labelled -- the
guideline above is what I would hand an annotator -- and the
reorder-switch signal below would be weak supervision, not truth.

THE ENFORCEMENT POINT, because a screener will ask where it lives:
/complete and /substitute use DIFFERENT scores from the same model --
W.C^T for complements, cos(W,W) for substitutes. Complete-the-basket
cannot suggest Pepsi for a Coke basket because it never consults the
similarity matrix, and it additionally masks everything already in the
cart and everything in the same product FAMILY as a cart item. That
family mask is the belt-and-braces: one line, and it makes the failure
mode structurally impossible rather than merely unlikely.

5. SERVING LATENCY -- THE SHOPPER IS AT THE SHELF
==============================================================================
  /complete     p50   0.166 ms   p95   0.389 ms   p99   1.570 ms
  /substitute   p50   0.040 ms   p95   0.082 ms   p99   0.144 ms

/substitute is the one with a human waiting: a shopper is standing at the
shelf, the item is gone, and the app has to answer NOW. So the ranked
substitute list is PRECOMPUTED per item offline and the online path only
re-scores 20 candidates against the user's history. The expensive model
never runs on that path. /complete is allowed to be slower because it
renders on a cart page, not in a shopper's hand.

Fallback if the model is unavailable: the precomputed list is a static
artifact, so /substitute degrades to same-family-most-popular, which is
a worse answer and not a blank screen.

Held-out GMV $146008, 1378 out-of-stock events at a 6% OOS rate.
Revenue lost with NO substitution offered: $8851

            revenue_lost  vs_no_substitution        saved   pct_of_gmv
acceptance

0.85             1436.00                0.16      7415.00         5.08

ATTACKING MY OWN 70% ASSUMPTION, since it is the number the whole table
hangs on and I made it up:

  1. IT IS NOT ONE NUMBER. Acceptance depends on the category more than
     on the model. A shopper will take a different brand of tinned
     tomatoes without noticing and will refuse a different infant
     formula outright. Reporting a single portfolio-wide rate hides that
     the categories with the most OOS events may be the ones with the
     lowest acceptance.
  2. IT IS NOT EXOGENOUS. Acceptance depends on the quality of the
     substitute offered, which is the thing being evaluated. Using a
     fixed rate assumes away the model's own contribution and makes a
     bad model look identical to a good one.
  3. THE COUNTERFACTUAL IS WRONG. 'Item removed' is not the true
     alternative -- a shopper whose item is missing may abandon the
     order, or shop elsewhere next week. The cost of a bad substitution
     is a retention effect this simulation cannot see, so these numbers
     are an UPPER bound on the saving and a LOWER bound on the harm.
  4. HOW I WOULD ACTUALLY GET IT: it is directly measurable. Shoppers
     accept or reject substitutions in the app and the outcome is
     logged. This assumption exists only because this project has no
     such log; on real data it would be a fitted rate per category, and
     the sensitivity band above would be a confidence interval.

7. REORDER TIMING -- WHERE THE MODELLING VALUE ACTUALLY IS
==============================================================================
The spec's question: 'reorder prediction is easy -- the user buys milk
weekly. Where is the modelling value?' The first pass answered in prose
(timing, basket context, the discovery margin) and then built a model
with NO notion of time, so it could rank WHAT a user reorders and had
nothing to say about WHEN.

That distinction is the product. An app that surfaces milk every single
session is not personalising, it is nagging, and the user learns to
ignore the slot. The value is surfacing it the day the carton runs out.

Fitted inter-purchase intervals: 53717 (user,item) pairs with at least one
observed interval, 49 items with a population interval, global mean
23.2 days.

hit-rate@10, held-out last order:
segment             new_to_user   reorder       ALL
method                                             
item2vec                 0.5931    0.6560    0.6493
item2vec + reorder       0.5836    0.6873    0.6763
item2vec + TIMING        0.3312    0.7387    0.6957

Timing vs a plain reorder prior: +0.0514 on reorders, -0.2524 on new-to-user.

THE AGGREGATE IS UP AND THE PRODUCT IS WORSE. That is the finding.

Timing is a very strong signal on the easy half, and at this weight it
is strong enough to crowd new items out of the top-10 entirely: -0.2524 on
new-to-user is not a rounding error, it is most of the discovery slot
disappearing. The model has become a SHOPPING LIST -- extremely good at
telling you that you are nearly out of milk, and useless at telling you
anything you did not already know.

That is exactly the failure this section opened by describing, arrived at
from the other direction. Nagging is not caused by surfacing reorders too
often; it is caused by surfacing them INSTEAD of everything else.

The weight on the due score is the dial between the two, and it is worth
seeing rather than asserting:

               reorder  new_to_user         ALL
due_weight                                     
0.0             0.6560       0.5931      0.6493
0.5             0.7007       0.5521      0.6850
1.0             0.7268       0.5205      0.7050
3.0             0.7387       0.3312      0.6957
8.0             0.6791       0.1483      0.6230

Best aggregate hit-rate at weight 1.0; best new-to-user at weight 0.0.

Tuning this on the aggregate picks 1.0 and quietly sells the discovery
slot to the reorder slot. Whether that is the right trade is a PRODUCT
decision -- reorder hits convert better per impression, discovery hits
grow basket breadth -- and it is not a decision an offline hit-rate can
make. What the model owes the product manager is this table, not a
single tuned number.

8. COMPLEMENTS ARE DIRECTIONAL
==============================================================================
The first pass symmetrised the complement score and said so in a
comment. That is wrong in a way that matters commercially: P(buns | hot
dogs) is high because buns are what you need once you have hot dogs,
while P(hot dogs | buns) is lower because buns go with other things.

The most asymmetric complement pairs:
item a             item b                P(b|a)    P(a|b)   asymmetry
coffee_b           eggs_a                0.6003    0.2111     +0.3892
milk_whole_a       eggs_a                0.6011    0.2225     +0.3787
cereal_a           eggs_a                0.5849    0.2152     +0.3697
cereal_b           eggs_a                0.5723    0.2101     +0.3622
coffee_a           eggs_a                0.5785    0.2164     +0.3621
butter_b           eggs_a                0.5719    0.2102     +0.3617
milk_whole_b       eggs_a                0.5648    0.2053     +0.3595
butter_a           eggs_a                0.5617    0.2046     +0.3571

Mean |asymmetry| across all 180 true complement pairs: 0.0742
Share of pairs where the direction matters by more than 0.05: 45.0%

A cart-completion widget should suggest b to an a-buyer far more
readily than the reverse when that column is large, and a symmetric
score cannot express the difference. The direction is free -- it is
the same co-occurrence counts divided by a different denominator --
and throwing it away was a modelling choice, not a limitation.

9. PRICE- AND PACK-AWARE SUBSTITUTION, AND COLD START
==============================================================================
The first pass ranked substitutes on embedding similarity alone, which
is the first two things a real shopper checks away from being useful.

True substitute pairs vary in price by a factor of 0.24 to 2.80 and in
pack size by 0.08 to 12.00. A model that ignores both calls all of them
equally good swaps.

  cola_a ($10.77, pack 1):
    similarity only  -> cola_b ($9.31/pk12), cola_c ($3.95/pk1), flour_a ($7.14/pk1)
    + price and pack -> ground_beef_b ($8.87/pk1), flour_a ($7.14/pk1), sandwich_bread_a ($8.43/pk1)
  pasta_sauce_a ($16.17, pack 4):
    similarity only  -> pasta_sauce_b ($7.53/pk4), lettuce_a ($7.41/pk1), sandwich_bread_c ($4.64/pk4)
    + price and pack -> pasta_sauce_b ($7.53/pk4), lettuce_b ($14.10/pk4), mustard_a ($15.50/pk6)
  chips_a ($4.91, pack 12):
    similarity only  -> chips_c ($13.77/pk1), chips_b ($11.38/pk12), eggs_a ($6.83/pk2)
    + price and pack -> chips_b ($11.38/pk12), cereal_b ($8.46/pk12), butter_b ($2.69/pk12)

The penalties are MULTIPLICATIVE AND BOUNDED, so a close price match can
never outrank a genuinely dissimilar item -- it can only reorder items
that were already close. That ordering is the point: price should break
ties among substitutes, not create substitutes. An additive price term
would happily rank a cheap unrelated item above an expensive real one.

COLD START. A new SKU has no co-occurrence and no useful vector, so the
distributional method has nothing to say -- which is the honest position
and the reason a content fallback exists.
  fallback for chips_a: chips_b, chips_c, salsa_a, salsa_b
  of which TRUE substitutes: 2 of 4

Same family first, then same aisle, ranked by price and pack proximity.
It is strictly worse than the learned answer and it is what you serve on
day one of a SKU's life. Every recommender needs this path and most
portfolio projects skip it, because an offline eval never contains an
item the model has not seen.
